# Problem Brief

Healthcare generates an enormous amount of information about a person over their lifetime, yet very little of that information actually stays with the person in a usable form.

Every doctor consultation, diagnostic test, prescription, hospital admission, scan, health check-up, insurance claim, wearable reading and lifestyle change adds another piece to someone's health history. But these pieces rarely come together.

Instead, a person's health is scattered across hospital systems, diagnostic labs, paper files, PDFs, email, WhatsApp conversations, patient portals, insurance documents, wearable applications and, very often, their own memory.

The result is that the individual—the one person present throughout the entire healthcare journey—often has the least complete understanding of their own health.

This creates several connected problems.

## 1. A person's medical history is fragmented

There is no reliable, continuously maintained personal health history that follows most people throughout their lives.

A blood test may exist as a PDF from one laboratory. A scan may be stored in another hospital's system. A prescription may be sitting in WhatsApp. Older records may exist only on paper. Another hospital may maintain an entirely separate patient profile. Wearable information lives inside yet another application.

Even when records have been digitised, they are usually only digitally stored fragments.

They are not connected.

Over several years this becomes hundreds of individual documents, reports and data points spread across different providers and platforms.

When the information is eventually needed, the patient has to reconstruct their own history.

They search old emails, find previous PDFs, scroll through WhatsApp, remember which hospital performed a test, carry physical files, or simply rely on memory.

Important information can consequently be forgotten, misplaced or permanently lost.

## 2. Medical records provide data, but very little understanding

Most healthcare records are designed for the immediate clinical encounter in which they were created.

A laboratory report tells a patient what their values are today.

It usually does not tell them:

- how those values have changed over the last three years,
- whether a gradual trend is developing,
- whether the value is unusual compared with their own historical baseline,
- what other parts of their health history may provide useful context,
- or what information may be worth discussing during their next medical consultation.

The same problem exists across prescriptions, diagnoses, imaging reports, hospitalisation records and health check-ups.

Every document becomes another isolated snapshot.

Humans therefore accumulate health data without accumulating an equivalent understanding of their health.

The longer the history becomes, the more difficult it becomes for an ordinary person to interpret manually.

## 3. Health records are organised around institutions rather than the person

Healthcare systems naturally organise information around hospitals, departments, visits and documents.

But people do not experience their health that way.

A single health issue can span years, multiple doctors, several laboratories and different hospitals.

For example, one condition may involve an initial consultation, several blood tests, medication changes, a specialist referral, imaging, another consultation six months later and periodic monitoring afterwards.

Those records may exist in completely different places.

The patient is expected to understand that they are related.

This means the user effectively becomes the integration layer between healthcare providers.

And solving this cannot simply mean giving users another folder system where they manually categorise files. Most people do not know how to classify medical information correctly, and they should not need to understand medical terminology just to maintain their health history.

The information needs to make sense around the person, condition, health area and episode of care, rather than around where a PDF happens to have been stored.

## 4. Healthcare follow-ups depend too heavily on human memory

A significant amount of healthcare does not end when the patient leaves the doctor's room.

A doctor might recommend:

- repeating a test after several months,
- monitoring a particular measurement,
- returning for a follow-up consultation,
- completing a course of medication,
- undergoing periodic screening,
- or checking something again if symptoms continue.

But once the consultation is over, responsibility for remembering those instructions often falls almost entirely on the patient.

Months later, the prescription may no longer be readily available and the person may not remember exactly what was recommended.

Routine preventive healthcare faces a similar problem.

Most people do not maintain a structured understanding of what health checks they have completed, what may be due next, or what previously identified issues should still be monitored.

As a result, healthcare becomes unnecessarily reactive.

People often return to their health information after something goes wrong, rather than having continuity between medical events.

## 5. Every new doctor often starts with an incomplete picture

When visiting a new doctor, patients are frequently asked to explain years of medical history from memory.

Previous diagnoses, medications, allergies, procedures, hospitalisations, abnormal test results and treatments may all be relevant.

But the patient may not remember the exact details.

Even when they possess the records, handing a doctor dozens of PDFs or a thick physical file does not automatically provide useful context.

Important information can be buried inside years of unrelated documents.

This creates friction for both sides.

The patient struggles to explain their history accurately, while the clinician spends valuable consultation time reconstructing information that already exists somewhere.

The problem is therefore not simply access to records.

It is the ability to surface the right history, in the right context, at the right moment.

## 6. Everyday health information is disconnected from clinical health

A person's health does not exist only during hospital visits.

Sleep, resting heart rate, activity, exercise, weight, nutrition, symptoms, stress and other everyday signals can provide useful context around longer-term health.

Increasingly, some of this information is already being captured through phones and wearable devices.

But it exists in a separate world from medical records.

A wearable application may know what someone's sleep has looked like for six months while their medical history contains laboratory results, prescriptions and consultations from the same period.

Neither side meaningfully understands the other.

The individual once again has to make the connection manually.

This means potentially useful longitudinal context is generated continuously but rarely becomes part of an understandable personal health picture.

## 7. People do not have a continuous way to understand what is happening between medical visits

Health changes do not happen only when a test is performed.

People experience symptoms, changes in energy, sleep disruption, digestive issues, recurring pain and many other changes between consultations.

Most of this information is either forgotten or recorded informally.

By the time the person eventually sees a doctor, they may struggle to explain when something began, how frequently it occurred or whether anything else changed during the same period.

The result is another broken timeline.

Clinical records capture healthcare events.

Wearables capture certain physiological and activity signals.

The person experiences everything in between.

But these three views are rarely connected.

## 8. Health insurance introduces an entirely separate layer of complexity

Medical care and health insurance are closely related in the real world, yet they are usually managed as completely separate systems.

People are expected to understand lengthy insurance policies, exclusions, waiting periods, coverage limits, room-rent restrictions, claim requirements, network hospitals and other conditions—often precisely when they or a family member are already dealing with a medical situation.

The complexity increases further because many people have more than one form of coverage.

A person may simultaneously have:

- an employer-provided group health policy,
- a personal health insurance policy,
- and a top-up or super top-up policy.

Understanding which policy should be used, what each policy covers, how benefits interact and what documents are required can become a problem of its own.

Users may unnecessarily pay expenses themselves, use the wrong policy first, fail to utilise available coverage, or struggle through claim documentation after treatment.

Managing the financial side of healthcare therefore becomes another fragmented process that the patient has to coordinate manually.

## 9. Insurance claims can create a second problem: unnecessary exposure of medical history

When making a claim, patients frequently have to assemble hospital bills, prescriptions, investigation reports, discharge summaries and other documents.

But a health record contains far more information than an insurer necessarily needs for a particular claim.

Without careful controls, making healthcare information easier to aggregate could accidentally make it easier to over-share it as well.

A user seeking reimbursement for one episode of care should not automatically expose their entire historical medical profile simply because the information exists in the same system.

The requirement is therefore more nuanced than simply making health information shareable.

People need to be able to disclose the information required for a particular purpose without unnecessarily surrendering the rest of their medical history.

This becomes especially important if insurers, employers, healthcare providers or other organisations eventually participate in the ecosystem.

## 10. The underlying problem is ownership and continuity

Hospitals maintain hospital records.

Laboratories maintain laboratory reports.

Insurance companies maintain policy and claim information.

Wearable companies maintain device data.

Each organisation sees the portion of the person's health journey that passes through its system.

But the individual lives through all of it.

Today, there is rarely a layer whose primary responsibility is maintaining that individual's continuous, understandable and user-controlled health history across organisations and across time.

That is the fundamental gap.

A person's health information may technically exist, but it does not necessarily exist for the person.

## The Core Problem

The problem is therefore not a lack of health data.

We already generate enormous amounts of it.

The problem is that this information remains fragmented, static, difficult to understand and disconnected from future action.

People are effectively left to act as their own health-information managers:

> collecting records → remembering history → comparing reports → tracking changes → remembering follow-ups → preparing for consultations → understanding insurance → coordinating claims → deciding what information to share.

These are complex responsibilities being placed on ordinary users who neither have the time nor the medical knowledge to manage them properly.

What is missing is continuity.

A person should not have to reconstruct their health every time they need healthcare.

Their health history should already exist as a continuously organised, understandable and privately controlled picture that grows with them throughout their life.

> **In short: healthcare remembers encounters. It does not reliably remember the person.**
