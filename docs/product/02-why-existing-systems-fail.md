# Why Existing Systems Fail

The healthcare industry already has electronic medical records, hospital portals, government health infrastructure, diagnostic applications, insurance platforms, wearable ecosystems and personal health record products.

The problem is not that technology is absent.

The problem is that almost every existing system was built to solve one part of the healthcare journey for one stakeholder.

- Hospitals build systems to operate hospitals.
- Laboratories build systems to deliver test results.
- Insurers build systems to administer policies and claims.
- Wearable companies build systems around their devices.
- Government infrastructure focuses on interoperability and access.
- Consumer health applications usually focus on storage, appointments, wellness or a narrow category of healthcare.

Each system can work perfectly within its own boundary while the individual's overall experience remains fragmented.

That is why digitising healthcare has not automatically created a coherent personal health system.

## 1. Electronic medical records are provider-centric

Hospital information systems and electronic medical records are primarily operational systems for healthcare providers.

They are designed around:

- patients registered within that organisation,
- appointments and encounters,
- clinical documentation,
- billing,
- prescriptions,
- orders,
- investigations,
- procedures,
- and internal workflows.

They are extremely important infrastructure.

But they are not designed to become the individual's lifelong health system.

A hospital generally has a strong view of what happened inside that hospital.

It may have little or no visibility into what happened at another hospital, an independent laboratory, a pharmacy, a wearable platform or a previous provider.

When the person changes healthcare providers, the continuity often breaks.

The patient's life is longitudinal.

The provider's record is institutional.

Those are fundamentally different models.

## 2. Patient portals expose records without solving fragmentation

Hospital and laboratory portals improve access, but access alone does not create understanding.

A patient may now be able to download their blood test or discharge summary instead of collecting it physically.

That is useful.

But the result is often still another PDF.

Another portal.

Another login.

Another isolated piece of medical history.

The patient eventually accumulates a collection of digital documents rather than a coherent health history.

This is an important distinction:

> **Digitisation solves paper. It does not automatically solve fragmentation.**

## 3. Interoperability infrastructure moves records; it does not necessarily make them useful

Healthcare interoperability is an essential part of solving this problem.

Systems that allow health information to move between providers can dramatically improve healthcare infrastructure.

But interoperability alone addresses the question:

> "Can this information be exchanged?"

It does not completely answer:

> "Can this person understand and continuously use their health history?"

Even when records from multiple institutions become accessible, the individual may still receive:

- multiple documents,
- duplicate information,
- medical terminology they do not understand,
- conflicting formats,
- disconnected episodes,
- and years of data without meaningful longitudinal context.

Transporting information and organising information are different problems.

A successful consumer health layer requires both.

## 4. Most personal health record products behave like digital filing cabinets

Many consumer health record products improve the storage problem.

Users can upload reports, prescriptions and other documents into one application.

That is valuable, but storage alone has a ceiling.

If the user uploads fifty laboratory reports and twenty prescriptions, they may simply have created a better-organised version of the folder that already existed on their phone.

The fundamental questions remain:

- What changed?
- What matters?
- What needs to be followed up?
- Which records relate to the same problem?
- What should be discussed with a doctor?
- What happened between two medical events?
- What information is actually relevant right now?

A document repository answers:

> "Where is my report?"

A true personal health system must also help answer:

> "What does my health history tell me?"

## 5. Existing systems usually organise documents, not health

Most systems naturally organise information using structures such as:

> Hospital → Visit → Document

or:

> Folder → Report → Date

But human health does not follow those boundaries.

Consider one ongoing issue.

It might involve:

- symptoms beginning at home,
- a primary-care consultation,
- a laboratory test elsewhere,
- medication,
- a specialist consultation,
- another laboratory,
- a scan,
- a medication change,
- wearable changes,
- and another follow-up six months later.

Those records belong to one health story.

Yet existing systems frequently treat them as unrelated events because they were created by different organisations.

The user is left to understand the relationship manually.

This is one of the deepest limitations of today's systems:

> **they store healthcare events without reconstructing the health journey connecting them.**

## 6. Search is not the same as intelligence

Some products make records searchable.

Others provide dashboards or allow users to ask questions about uploaded documents.

These are useful improvements, but they can still leave most of the cognitive work with the user.

The person must know what to search for.

They must know which question to ask.

They must recognise that two pieces of information separated by eighteen months might be related.

They must remember that a doctor asked them to repeat a particular test.

They must notice that a measurement has gradually changed over several reports.

A useful health system cannot depend entirely on the user already knowing what they should investigate.

It has to help surface relevant information proactively while remaining appropriately cautious about medical interpretation.

## 7. Most systems are episodic rather than continuous

Traditional healthcare technology becomes active when something happens.

A consultation occurs.

A test is ordered.

A prescription is written.

A hospitalisation takes place.

A claim is submitted.

Then the interaction ends.

But health continues between these events.

Someone might experience recurring headaches for six weeks.

Their sleep may deteriorate.

Their resting heart rate may change.

Their weight might move significantly.

They may begin a new medication.

They may feel better after changing part of their lifestyle.

Most clinical systems know almost nothing about this period because there was no clinical encounter.

Wearable systems may capture part of it, but without the medical context.

The result is two separate timelines:

> **clinical life and everyday life.**

Existing systems rarely join them.

## 8. Wearable platforms understand signals, not the complete medical history

Wearables have dramatically increased the amount of personal health data available.

They can track information such as:

- sleep,
- activity,
- heart rate,
- exercise,
- recovery,
- and other physiological signals.

But wearable ecosystems typically begin with the device.

They generally do not contain the individual's complete medical history, previous investigations, medications, hospitalisations and clinical context.

A wearable might detect a change.

A medical record might contain information relevant to interpreting that change.

But the two usually exist separately.

The potential value lies not merely in collecting more data, but in placing everyday signals within appropriate longitudinal health context.

## 9. Healthcare applications usually require the user to initiate everything

Most systems wait for the user to act.

The user must:

- remember that a follow-up is due,
- open the application,
- locate the relevant record,
- compare old and new results,
- decide that something has changed,
- remember what the doctor previously said,
- and determine what to do next.

This is essentially digital administration.

It is better than paper administration, but the burden still exists.

A healthcare system capable of maintaining continuity should reduce this cognitive load.

- The system should understand that a doctor requested a follow-up.
- It should recognise that the follow-up has not occurred.
- It should understand that a new report relates to previous reports.
- It should make the relevant context available when the user needs it.

The user should not have to repeatedly reconstruct their own healthcare workflow.

## 10. Clinical summaries are usually created for individual encounters

Doctors routinely create summaries, discharge documents and clinical notes.

But these generally describe one consultation or one admission.

What is often missing is a dynamically maintained summary of the person across time.

Someone who has accumulated ten years of healthcare records does not necessarily have one reliable place answering:

- What significant medical events have happened?
- What medications were previously used?
- What allergies are known?
- What ongoing issues are being monitored?
- Which findings changed over time?
- What follow-ups remain outstanding?
- What happened during previous episodes of the same problem?

This information exists somewhere.

The problem is assembling it.

## 11. Insurance systems begin with the policy rather than the person's healthcare journey

Health insurers have built sophisticated systems for policy administration and claims.

But the consumer experience usually begins with insurance terminology:

- sum insured,
- deductibles,
- waiting periods,
- exclusions,
- network hospitals,
- sub-limits,
- co-payments,
- room-rent limits,
- top-ups,
- reimbursement,
- pre-authorisation.

The individual has to translate a real healthcare situation into that policy structure.

The problem becomes significantly harder when the person has multiple policies.

Someone may simultaneously hold:

- employer group insurance,
- an individual or family policy,
- and a top-up or super top-up.

These systems generally administer each policy independently.

The consumer is left to work out how the policies should interact.

The real question the person wants answered is often not:

> "What does clause 7.3 of this policy mean?"

It is:

> "I am being admitted tomorrow. What am I covered for, which policy should I use first, what might I need to pay, and what documents do I need?"

Existing insurance infrastructure was not primarily designed to answer the question from that perspective.

## 12. Medical records and insurance are treated as separate worlds

The same hospitalisation generates two parallel processes.

**Healthcare process**
Diagnosis, tests, medication, procedure, discharge and follow-up.

**Insurance process**
Pre-authorisation, policy validation, documentation, claim filing, settlement and reimbursement.

For the patient, these are part of the same event.

For technology systems, they are often completely separate.

The individual once again becomes the coordinator.

They have to understand:

- what happened medically,
- what documents prove it,
- which documents the insurer requires,
- what should be submitted,
- which policy applies,
- what has already been paid,
- and whether anything remains claimable elsewhere.

This artificial separation creates enormous unnecessary administrative burden.

## 13. More centralisation can create new privacy problems

A comprehensive personal health system creates value precisely because it can see information across time.

But that also creates risk.

If a platform combines:

- previous diagnoses,
- medications,
- hospitalisations,
- investigations,
- genetic information,
- wearable data,
- symptoms,
- insurance information,
- and lifestyle data,

then indiscriminate sharing becomes dangerous.

Many systems treat sharing as a document-management problem:

> select file → share file.

But health privacy requires something more nuanced:

> **share only the information appropriate for the purpose.**

- A doctor may need a broader medical history.
- An insurer processing one claim may need documentation relating primarily to that episode.
- A family member might need only emergency information.
- A fitness professional may need something entirely different.

The existence of a complete health history should not mean that every recipient gains access to the complete health history.

Centralisation without purpose-based disclosure simply replaces fragmentation with overexposure.

## 14. Most AI health products begin with the chatbot

Generative AI has produced a new class of healthcare applications built around conversational interfaces.

A user describes symptoms or asks a medical question and the system responds.

Conversation can be extremely useful.

But a chatbot without a reliable longitudinal health model faces a fundamental limitation.

It knows what the user tells it during the conversation.

It may not know:

- what happened three years ago,
- what medications were previously prescribed,
- how laboratory values changed over time,
- what a doctor asked the person to repeat,
- whether a similar symptom occurred previously,
- or what changed in wearable measurements around that period.

The highest-value use of AI in personal health is therefore not simply:

> AI that can talk about health.

It is:

> **AI that can reason over a structured, permissioned and longitudinal understanding of the person's own health information.**

The conversation interface is only the surface.

The health history underneath it is the real asset.

## 15. Users are still forced to become their own health-information manager

Across all these systems, the same pattern appears.

The user remains responsible for connecting everything.

- They must remember which provider has which record.
- They must keep track of follow-ups.
- They must compare reports.
- They must remember past treatments.
- They must prepare their own history for a new doctor.
- They must correlate lifestyle changes with health changes.
- They must understand their insurance.
- They must assemble claims.
- They must decide what information to disclose.

Different applications may assist with individual steps.

But the responsibility for maintaining continuity still belongs to the individual.

That is the fundamental failure.

## The Structural Gap

Existing healthcare systems are primarily built around four things:

> institutions, transactions, documents and episodes.

What is missing is a system built around:

> **the person, over time.**

This explains why no single improvement—digitising records, introducing interoperability, adding cloud storage, connecting wearables or putting a chatbot on top—completely solves the problem.

Each addresses one layer.

The unresolved challenge is connecting all of those layers into one continuously maintained model of the person's health while preserving user control over what information leaves that model.

The difference can be expressed simply:

| Existing systems | What is still missing |
| --- | --- |
| Store → Retrieve → Display | Collect → Understand → Connect → Remember → Surface → Act → Protect |

The opportunity is therefore not to build another hospital portal, document vault, wellness tracker, insurance application or medical chatbot.

It is to build the personal continuity layer that sits across all of them.

- Hospitals can remain the system of record for healthcare delivery.
- Laboratories can remain the system of record for investigations.
- Insurers can remain the system of record for coverage and claims.
- Wearables can remain the system of record for continuous physiological data.

But the individual still needs a system whose responsibility is something none of them were fundamentally designed to provide:

> **maintaining an understandable, actionable and user-controlled picture of that person's health across their entire life.**
